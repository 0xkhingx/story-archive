There is a scene that refuses to cut. A car under attack, the camera trapped inside it, turning in small panicked arcs while everything goes wrong at once. No edit to hide behind. No relief. You sit in it for as long as they do.

I did not know it at the time, but that scene was teaching me something about attention. A cut is a mercy the director grants you — look away, breathe, reset. When the mercy is withheld, you have to stay. And staying changes what a scene means.

<MarginNote>
Cuts are where films usually lie to you. This one decided to tell the truth for four minutes straight.
</MarginNote>

The film is about the end of the future — no children, no forward — and yet its most famous images are all about carrying something fragile through chaos without putting it down. A woman. A baby. A cause. The camera behaves the way hope behaves in that world: it does not look away, even when looking hurts.

<PullLine>Hope is not optimism. It is attention that refuses to cut away.</PullLine>

That is what stayed with me, and it leaked into ordinary life. Traffic. Queues. Difficult conversations. The instinct is always to cut — check the phone, change the subject, leave the room. The long take argues, without a single speech, that remaining is itself a position. You do not have to fix the scene. You only have to not abandon it.

I think about that shot whenever I want to look away from something that matters. Stay in the car. Keep the camera running. See what happens if you do not cut.
