It had rained the way Lagos rains when it means it — sudden, total, gone in an hour — and the road was doing that glossy orange-mirror thing it does at sunset. I squeezed into a danfo going my direction, notebook in my lap, headphones on but nothing playing. I liked to sit and watch the city file past like footage.

Two stops in, a man stood up near the front and started preaching. Not the aggressive kind. Conversational, almost. Like he had decided the bus was his living room and we were all family who had stayed too long without talking.

His text, if you could call it that, was about patience. He said — and I wrote this down verbatim because I did not trust memory with it — “God is not in a hurry, that is why He can wait inside traffic with you.”

<MarginNote>
I have forgotten entire semesters of school. I remember this sentence from a stranger, word for word, years later.
</MarginNote>

The whole bus laughed at that. Not at him — with him. The conductor laughed. The woman selling plantain chips by the window laughed. For about ninety seconds, a danfo in Lagos traffic was the warmest room in the city.

<PullLine>Some sermons need a cathedral. His just needed gridlock.</PullLine>

<StoryPanel
  src="/images/environments/danfo-empty.webp"

I think about that ride whenever patience feels like passivity. It is not. The man was not resigned to traffic; he was *hosting* inside it. He took the exact conditions everyone was suffering and turned them into the venue.

That is what stayed. Not theology — logistics. If you cannot leave the room, you can still decide what kind of room it is.
